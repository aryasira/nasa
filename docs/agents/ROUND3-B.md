## 1. The problem

Below the simulator the page is about **1,800 words in ten stacked, identical, grey rounded boxes**.
It reads like a report generated by an AI, not a tool a person designed. Specifically:
- Every section is the same card with the same heading style, so nothing stands out.
- The answer (a 10-day slip cuts both-in-view time from 155 h to 82 h) sits in a sentence. It should be the biggest thing on the page.
- The "Options" table has two or three full sentences of cause text under every row, so the ranking is buried.
- The landing-date chart is followed by three paragraphs that repeat what the chart already shows.
- Legends are long text strings ("Earth above the local horizon (hatched)").
- Compare sites is a wall of 21 checkboxes.
- Footnotes are everywhere.

## 2. Goal

**Show, don't tell.** Someone should get the story in five seconds without reading a sentence:
*the slip, what it costs, why, and what to do instead.*
Words are for labels, units and a short "why". Long explanations go behind a disclosure (`<details>`) or a tooltip, or into Explain mode. Nothing true gets deleted.

**Word budget: at most about 250 visible words below the simulator by default** (from about 1,800 now).
Count them with `document.querySelector('#h-brief').parentElement.innerText`, or whatever the container is, and put the before and after numbers in the PR.

## 3. Research first (30 to 45 min, then commit to one direction)

Look at real, human-designed data and engineering interfaces. Pick **one** direction and write it down in `docs/design.md` (about 15 lines: references, type, colour, grid, 2 or 3 rules). Places worth looking:
- Mission-control and flight-dynamics displays, technical drawings, and the style of the old printed lunar charts. The point is the feel only: **no NASA or agency logos or insignia.**
- Data-journalism visual essays (for example The Pudding, the Financial Times, Reuters Graphics, the New York Times Upshot): big single numbers, annotated charts, small multiples.
- Explorable explanations (Bret Victor, Nicky Case, Bartosz Ciechanowski): the chart *is* the explanation.
- Hardware and instrument UI (for example Teenage Engineering, cockpit glass displays, oscilloscopes): dense but calm, monospace numbers, clear states.
- Swiss/International typographic style: a strict grid, left-aligned, asymmetric, generous whitespace, few weights.

Write down which references you actually looked at (titles and URLs) in `docs/design.md`. Do not copy any one site's layout, logo or artwork.

## 4. What "less vibe-coded, more human" means here (checklist)

Avoid the generated-UI tells:
- no row after row of identical rounded cards with soft shadows
- no grey-on-grey low-contrast text everywhere
- no emoji, and no sparkle or gradient decoration
- not every element the same size and weight
- no centred everything
- no ad-hoc legends that are full sentences

Do instead:
- **One strong grid** (for example 12 columns) with deliberate asymmetry: a wide chart column and a narrow annotation rail.
- **Typographic hierarchy:** one display face plus one monospace for numbers, used consistently (self-hosted or a system stack; no external font CDN). Very large numerals for the key results. Small caps or uppercase micro-labels for units.
- **Hairline rules and section numbers** (01, 02, 03…) instead of boxes. Use cards only where something really is a separate object.
- **A restrained palette:** the existing Sun amber, Earth blue and both-in-view green, plus one warning colour and neutrals. Keep the colour-blind-safe meanings. Never encode with colour alone; keep the hatching and the labels.
- **Annotations on the charts themselves** (a label with a leader line pointing at the thing) instead of paragraphs under them.
- Consistent units and rounding: hours with one decimal in tables, whole hours in prose, and the same everywhere.
- Small, deliberate details that show a human chose them: aligned tabular numbers, a scale bar, tick marks, a north arrow on the horizon plot, consistent 4/8 px spacing.
